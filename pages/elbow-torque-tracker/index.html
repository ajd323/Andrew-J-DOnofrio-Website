Situation

  • Transition Cornell DEBUT from a traditional student project model toward a structured medical device development process.
  • Train new engineers on electromechanical medical device development, documentation, and systems engineering practices.
  • Develop a Class II diagnostic platform capable of estimating valgus elbow torque during UCL rehabilitation.
  • Create verification and validation plans aligned with FDA design controls and applicable medical device standards.
  • Translate published Hill Musculoskeletal Models into a wearable, field-deployable product.

Outcomes

  • Led an 8-person multidisciplinary engineering team through three prototype generations and full system integration.
  • Achieved 2.58% torque estimation error compared to OptiTrack motion-capture ground truth.
  • Authored more than 100 pages of Design History File, regulatory, testing, and commercialization documentation.
  • Created over 200 technical onboarding slides and standardized development processes for future Cornell DEBUT teams.
  • Designed the custom PCB architecture, wearable hardware, embedded firmware, and cloud-based biomechanics analysis pipeline.
  • Submitted the project as a VentureWell DEBUT Phase II-B entry with a complete commercialization and regulatory strategy.

The Problem

UCL reconstruction surgeries in MLB pitchers rose 40% from 2010 to 2019, and recovery takes roughly 20.5 months on average. In New York State alone, UCL reconstruction procedures increased 343% between 2003 and 2014, with 88.5% occurring in athletes aged 15 to 24.

There is still no good way to measure how much stress the elbow is taking during that recovery outside a motion-capture lab. Lab-grade systems like KinaTrax's Hawkeye cost an estimated $1,000,000 to install and require intensive training data. Consumer wearables are far cheaper but not accurate enough to guide a return-to-throwing decision, the closest competitor runs 38.7% error against lab ground truth.

Our own surveys of 10+ clinical staff and D1 athletes confirmed an underdeveloped market for managing UCL-based physical therapy. I took this on as Project Manager for a new Cornell DEBUT team: build a device accurate enough to trust, worn comfortably enough to use every session, at a fraction of the cost of a motion-capture lab.

01
Background Sensing Approach

No cameras in the field, so torque comes from two on-body signals: surface EMG for muscle activation, IMUs for limb orientation. Both feed a patient-specific Hill's Musculoskeletal Model.

τ(θ, t) = Σ r(θ, t) · F(θ, t)

r is the moment arm from muscle to elbow joint, derived from IMU orientation. F is muscle force, derived from sEMG. Three muscle groups modeled: biceps brachii long head, triceps brachii long head, pronator teres, the dominant contributors to UCL loading, chosen to keep sensor count and calibration time practical outside a lab.

02
Prototyping Hardware Iterations
V1.0

Proof of Concept

Got sEMG, IMU, and the ESP32 talking on protoboard. The goal was a working signal chain, not a wearable yet.

V1.1

Custom PCB

Split into a main board (ESP32-WROOM, MCP73871 charging, microSD) and a peripheral board routing sensors through a TCA9548 I2C mux, since every sensor shares a default address.

V2.0

Validated System

Slimmer housings, 3.7V LiPo, full V&V configuration. This is the version behind every result on this page.

I designed the 2-layer PCB for both boards in KiCAD: analog filtering, USB-C charging, and I2C multiplexing across 10+ chips and 8 connectors. Housings were modeled in Fusion360 against a sleeve-fit chart built from forearm and bicep circumference data, X-small through X-large, so the band doesn't shift mid-throw.

Firmware on the ESP32 samples at ~10 kHz, filters and smooths the signal, and logs to microSD. Post-session, a Python pipeline I built and host on GitLab runs the Hill's Model calculation and pushes results to the dashboard.

Beyond the electronics, I designed the Axio Recovery logo and product mark used across the casing, packaging, and the IFU, then wrote the Instructions for Use itself: setup steps, sensor placement diagrams, calibration sequence, and warnings, the kind of document that has to be unambiguous for someone who has never seen the device before. I shot and edited the product photography used in the final report and competition materials, building a small lightbox setup to get clean, consistent shots of each prototype generation against a plain background. I also handled the physical assembly and packaging of the final V2.0 units for testing and demo days: laser-cutting and fitting the housings, routing internal wiring, and packaging each unit so it survived being handed off between team members and reviewers intact.

Software

I built and host the clinician-facing dashboard. Upload a session's sensor data, and the Hill's Model pipeline runs server-side, returning torque estimates, muscle activation traces, and session metrics through the browser. No local install, no specialized biomechanics software required.

03
Background Field Constraints

Athletic immersion is a structured field-research method I ran before any hardware existed: take TRIZ design parameters (moving-object length, stationary-object weight, applied force, ease of repair) and test them directly against real throwing motions instead of guessing at constraints from a desk. I organized and led sessions across baseball, tennis, and lacrosse, working with athletes and coaches on-site to observe how a sensor band would actually need to behave during a live throw, not a lab simulation.

I trained the rest of the team on the protocol so sessions stayed consistent across sports and athletes: what to measure, how to log observations, and how to translate a TRIZ parameter into a testable hardware constraint. That standardized process is what let us run sessions in parallel rather than serially, and it's the same protocol structure later teams can reuse.

The output was 18 concrete constraints on fit, mass, and attachment points, things like maximum band thickness before it interfered with elbow extension, or where Velcro would loosen under repeated throwing motion, that shaped the V1.0 architecture before we wrote a line of firmware. Catching those constraints this early avoided redesigns later: every later prototype generation built on the same fit envelope established here.

04
Testing Validation

Once V1.0 was working, I co-authored the experimental protocol for access to Cornell's motion capture studio: one pitcher, one catcher, one data collector, five infrared trackers placed at the shoulder, upper arm, elbow, wrist, and hand, and low-intensity overhand throws capped under 20 mph for safety. I ran the sessions myself, syncing Axio Recovery's onboard logging with the OptiTrack capture in real time so both systems recorded the same throws simultaneously. OptiTrack output was computed via the Upper Extremity Dynamic Model in OpenSim and used as ground truth against our Hill's Model estimate.

I also designed and ran the verification protocol separately from the accuracy study. Six participants completed full pitching regimens wearing the V2.0 band: an 8-hour continuous wear trial with zero electrical dropouts, no restriction in range of motion, a band under 75g, and surface temperature under 40°C throughout.

05
Impact Beyond the Device

Once V2.0 was validated, the team compiled results into a submission for the DEBUT VentureWell competition: the 2.58% accuracy result, the verification data, and a working demo, packaged into a Phase II-B entry. Putting that submission together meant translating engineering results into a case a non-technical reviewer could evaluate, which took as much work as the testing itself.

The business pitch and regulatory strategy behind that submission were my work. I formulated the commercialization pathway alongside the engineering: a 510(k) submission strategy under 21 CFR 890.1375 against the CMAP Pro™ predicate device, Remote Therapeutic Monitoring billing codes for reimbursement, and a manufacturing cost model targeting $549.99 retail against an estimated $353.73 in COGS. None of that changes the device, but a working prototype without a viable path to market doesn't go anywhere past the lab, and someone on the team had to own that case end to end.

From there, we began the process of applying to Cornell's Rev incubator program to take the next step past DEBUT: building out the business plan, refining the cost model with real supplier quotes, and preparing the device for evaluation by people outside the engineering team. That process is still ongoing, but the documentation, V&V record, and regulatory strategy built during this project are what make that next step possible.
